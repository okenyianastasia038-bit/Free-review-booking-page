# Case Study: Designing a Booking Page That Turns Store Owners Into Clients

**Project:** Free Website Review booking page
**Role:** UI/UX designer, copywriter and front-end developer (solo project)
**Timeline:** November 2026, about three weeks
**Tools:** Figma, Claude design canvas, Canva, HTML, CSS, JavaScript, FormSubmit, Calendly, GitHub Pages

---

## 1. The challenge

I help online stores turn more of their visitors into buyers. My first step with any new client is a free 30-minute review of their website. After the review, I offer a fixed-price full-site redesign.

My content on TikTok and LinkedIn was starting to reach store owners, but I had nowhere to send them. People had to message me directly, and many never did. I needed one page that would take a curious store owner and turn them into a booked review.

The page had to work for a very specific person: a busy, sceptical online store owner, often in another country, usually on a phone, who has seen plenty of “free audits” that turned into hard sales calls.

**The design question:**

> How might we help a busy store owner recognise their problem, trust a designer they have never met, and book a free review in under a minute?

## 2. My role

This was a solo project. I was the client and the designer at the same time, which meant I had to be strict about separating what I wanted to say from what store owners needed to hear.

I was responsible for:

- Research and proto-personas
- Positioning, offer design and all copy
- Information architecture and wireframes
- Visual design, illustration and motion
- The interactive prototype and the production page
- Accessibility, responsiveness and documentation

## 3. Research

### What I did

1. **Desk research** on why online shoppers leave stores without buying. Published e-commerce research, including the Baymard Institute’s long-running cart abandonment studies, consistently places the average cart abandonment rate at around 70%, with surprise costs, forced account creation and long or confusing checkouts among the most common reasons. Google’s mobile research found that more than half of mobile visits are abandoned when a page takes longer than three seconds to load.
2. **A heuristic review of 20 small online stores** across fashion, food and beauty, checking the homepage, a product page, the cart and the checkout on a phone.
3. **A competitive review** of how freelance designers and agencies present free audits and booking pages.
4. **Social listening** in public spaces where store owners talk about their websites, such as the Shopify Community forums, e-commerce groups on Reddit and comments on my own TikTok and LinkedIn posts.

### What I found

**Insight 1: The same five problems kept appearing.**
Across the 20 stores, the most common issues were carts left behind with no follow-up, checkouts with too many steps or surprise costs, slow image-heavy product pages, crowded mobile layouts with pop-ups covering content, and product pages that did not answer key questions such as sizing, delivery time or returns.

**Insight 2: Owners know *that* they are losing sales, not *why*.**
In forum threads and comments, store owners often quoted their traffic and sales numbers but struggled to explain where shoppers dropped off. They described problems in plain words: “people add to cart and disappear”, “mobile looks messy”. They never used terms like “conversion funnel” or “friction”.

**Insight 3: “Free” makes people suspicious.**
Owners often posted mixed feelings about free audits. The most repeated fear was a long call that turned into a sales pitch. The offers they spoke well of had a real person behind them, a clear scope and something useful to take away even without buying.

**Insight 4: Long forms and calendar embeds put people off.**
Competing pages often asked for revenue, budget and platform details, or dropped visitors straight into a calendar. That asks for a commitment before the owner knows whether the designer is any good.

**Insight 5: Most visits happen on phones.**
Traffic from TikTok and LinkedIn is mostly mobile, so the page had to be designed for a phone first and still feel premium on desktop.

## 5. Design decisions and why I made them

### Decision 1: Lead with the store owner’s outcome, not my services

**What I did:** The headline is “Your store gets visitors. Let’s turn more of them into buyers.”, not “UI/UX design services”.
**Why:** Research showed owners think in sales, not design. The headline starts from something they already know is true (they have visitors) and moves to what they want (buyers).

### Decision 2: Answer the cost question immediately

**What I did:** “No cost, no obligation” sits right next to the main button, and the third card promises fixes “whether you work with me or not”.
**Why:** Suspicion of free offers was the biggest barrier. Answering it before it becomes an objection keeps people moving.

### Decision 3: Show the problems as a detective pinboard

**What I did:** Five illustrated panels, each naming a common frustration in the owner’s own words, are pinned to a board and connected by red threads to a centre card naming me as the fix.
**Why:** Owners recognise problems faster than they read explanations. The pinboard turns lost sales into a mystery to solve, gives the page a memorable centrepiece, and visually connects every problem to one action. The threads drawing themselves on scroll tell the whole story of the page in about two seconds.
**What I considered instead:** A simple bullet list (clear but forgettable) and a carousel (hides most of the problems and adds taps).

### Decision 4: Make the offer concrete with three bold cards

**What I did:** Three tall cards in butter yellow, burgundy and blush pink, each with a line illustration, a large title, a short description and a link straight to the form.
**Why:** The first version used plain white cards that looked like every other agency site. Bold colour blocks inspired by modern product marketing make the offer feel tangible and give the page personality, while the dividers and consistent structure keep it easy to scan.

### Decision 5: Four fields, only three required

**What I did:** Name, email, store link and an optional “biggest frustration” question.
**Why:** The store link is all I need to prepare a useful review. The open question gives owners a voice and helps me focus the call, but making it optional keeps the form short for fast bookers. I removed budget, revenue and platform questions after the competitive review showed they made a free offer feel like a qualification test.

### Decision 6: A short form first, with Calendly as an option

**What I did:** The form is the main way to book, and I reply within a day with time options. Below it, a “Book a free review on Calendly” button lets owners who already feel ready pick a time themselves. It opens Calendly in a new tab rather than embedding a calendar in the page.
**Why:** An embedded calendar is heavy, often hard to use on phones, and asks for a time before most owners trust me. The form lets me look at the store before we speak. But some owners are ready to commit straight away, and making them wait for an email would slow them down. Offering both paths serves the careful reader and the fast booker without adding weight to the page.

### Decision 7: Every call to action goes to the same place

**What I did:** The header button, hero button, pinboard button and all three card links scroll to the form and put the cursor in the first field.
**Why:** One goal per page. Repeating the same action after every section means a visitor can book the moment they are convinced, without hunting for the form.

### Decision 8: A real face in two places

**What I did:** My photo appears at the centre of the pinboard and next to the “Who it’s for” statement.
**Why:** Owners consistently trust people more than faceless companies. Placing the photo at the centre of the pinboard makes me literally the answer to the problems around it.

### Decision 9: Warm, credible and a little playful

**What I did:** Cream background, burgundy and black for trust, butter and blush for warmth, a serif for headlines, a friendly sans-serif for reading, handwriting for captions and a poster face for the footer signature.
**Why:** The page needs to feel premium enough for a marketing lead to share with her founder, and human enough for a small founder to feel comfortable. The early tea green direction felt fresh but less serious, so I moved to burgundy as the main colour and kept the playful colours as supporting tones.

### Decision 10: Motion with a purpose, and a way to switch it off

**What I did:** Springy hover effects on buttons and cards, a staggered hero entrance, threads that draw on scroll and a footer name that ripples on hover. Everything turns off for visitors who prefer reduced motion.
**Why:** Motion makes the page feel alive and rewards exploration, but it must never slow anyone down or make anyone uncomfortable. All animation is CSS-based, with no libraries, so the page stays fast.

### Decision 11: Designed for phones first

**What I did:** On phones the hero button becomes full width, cards stack, the pinboard becomes a two-column grid with pins on each panel and the centre card at the bottom, and every touch target is at least 44px.
**Why:** Most visitors arrive from social apps on their phones. The complex desktop pinboard would be unreadable if simply shrunk, so it was redesigned for small screens instead.

### Decision 12: The page is the portfolio

**What I did:** Built the page with semantic HTML, strong contrast, labelled fields, a skip link and no frameworks.
**Why:** I sell better websites. A slow or inaccessible page would undermine everything it says.

## 7. Results

The page launched in November 2026, so it is too early for meaningful conversion data. Instead of guessing, I set clear targets and will update this section with real numbers after 90 days.

| Measure | Target | How it will be measured |
|---|---|---|
| Visitor-to-booking conversion rate | 5% or higher | Page visits vs requests received |
| Form completion rate | 60% or higher | Form starts vs requests sent |
| Booked reviews per month | 10 or more | Requests in the inbox |
| Review-to-redesign rate | 20% or higher | Paid redesigns vs completed reviews |
| Lighthouse accessibility score | 95 or higher | Lighthouse in Chrome DevTools |
| Mobile Largest Contentful Paint | Under 2.5 seconds | Lighthouse and PageSpeed Insights |

## 8. What I learned

1. **Use the customer’s words.** “Carts left behind” beats “cart abandonment optimisation” every time.
2. **Trust is designed, not claimed.** A photo, a name, a clear scope and an honest promise do more than badges or bold claims.
3. **Short forms get better leads.** One good open question tells me more than five dropdowns.
4. **Motion should tell the story.** The threads earn their place because they explain the page. Decoration that did not explain anything was cut.
5. **Being your own client is hard.** Writing personas and a brief for myself kept me from designing for my own taste instead of for store owners.
